# Heavy Slow Resistance (HSR) for Tendinopathy

**Purpose:** Heavy Slow Resistance loading to remodel a painful tendon, written here for patellar tendinopathy (the HSR principles carry over to other tendons), plus glute medius work for hip control and knee stability.

**Use it** when the Runner Profile records a tendinopathy and the active Phase is for rehab. Outside rehab, the general [strength-training.md](strength-training.md) framework replaces it, and the single-leg decline squat test below can continue as an early-warning check.

**Frequency:** 3 sessions a week during a dedicated rehab Phase; 2 a week as maintenance once symptom-free.

**Tempo (Slots 1 and 2):** **3 seconds down, 3 seconds up. No bounce, no pause at the bottom.** This is the load that remodels the tendon.

The weekly progression and pain-gating for a particular rehab Phase live in its Plan.

---

## Why this protocol

### Tendons remodel under heavy, slow load

- Tendinopathy is a **load problem, not a rest problem**: rest detrains the tendon without fixing it.
- Heavy resistance at a slow, controlled tempo stimulates collagen synthesis and improves tendon stiffness and load tolerance.
- Aggravating load differs from therapeutic load. Jumping, sprinting, downhill running and hill repeats (high strain rate) and long fatigued runs (high cyclic volume) provoke; heavy slow lifting heals.

### Glute medius work is part of the prescription

- Weak or uncontrolled hip abduction often contributes to, or causes, anterior knee pain in runners.
- It goes into every session, both as rehab and as long-term insurance, and continues after the rehab Phase ends.

## Session structure

Every session uses the same **5-slot template**: pick one item from each menu and rotate within each menu across sessions.

### Slot 1: Heavy bilateral knee-dominant (the HSR core)

**Tempo: 3 s down, 3 s up. No bounce, no pause.**

- Barbell back squat
- Leg press
- Hack squat
- Goblet or front squat

### Slot 2: Unilateral lower body

**Same 6-second tempo.** Start with the affected or weaker side, count its reps, and match the other side to it.

- Bulgarian split squat (prescribe as "X lb × 2 DB", one in each hand)
- Reverse lunge (DB or barbell)
- Walking lunge
- Step-up (18–20" box)
- Single-leg leg press

### Slot 3: Hip hinge

Normal tempo (2 s down, 1 s up).

- Trap-bar deadlift
- Romanian deadlift (barbell)
- Single-leg RDL (kettlebell or DB)
- Barbell hip thrust
- 45° hyperextension (weighted)

### Slot 4: Glute medius main

Moderate to heavy load, **3 × 8–12 per side**, leading with the weaker side.

- Standing cable hip abduction (ankle cuff, low pulley)
- Side-lying hip abduction with a dumbbell on the outer thigh
- Single-leg hip thrust (DB or barbell on the hip)
- Hip abduction machine

### Slot 5: Glute medius finisher

Lighter load, **12–20 per side**, control-focused.

- Side plank with top-leg abduction (add a DB to progress)
- Banded lateral walks, 15 steps each direction
- Clamshell (band, top knee raised, hip neutral)
- Lateral step-up onto an 18" box

### Optional add-on

Standing or seated calf raises, **2 × 10–15.** Not required, but useful for runners.

## Understanding RM (repetition maximum)

**RM** expresses load relative to the runner's strength rather than as a fixed weight.

- **1RM:** the heaviest weight lifted for exactly one clean rep.
- **15RM:** the heaviest weight that completes 15 reps; rep 16 would fail.
- **6RM:** the heaviest for 6 reps.

"3 sets of 15 at 15RM" means a weight where the 15th rep is genuinely the last clean one. If rep 15 felt like five more were in the tank, the weight was too light: add load on the next set.

There's no need to test a 1RM. Estimate by feel and refine across sessions; within a week or two the runner knows their RM for each lift.

---

## HSR loading progression (Slots 1 and 2; Slot 3 optional)

Slots 4 and 5 progress by feel: add weight or band tension once every rep is clean.

| Weeks | Load | Sets × reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15RM | 3 × 15 |
| 2–3 | 12RM | 3 × 12 |
| 4–5 | 10RM | 4 × 10 |
| 6–8 | 8RM | 4 × 8 |
| 9–12 | 6RM | 4 × 6 |

**Pain during lifting:** up to **3/10 during the lift is acceptable** if it settles by the next morning. If it's sharper than that, or worse session to session, **drop back one step** (e.g. from 10RM to 12RM for a week).

## The single-leg decline squat test

A standard clinical assessment for patellar tendinopathy, used both to confirm the tendon is the source of pain and as a **weekly progress tracker**. The decline puts the knee into deeper flexion at a given depth, loading the patellar tendon more than a flat squat.

### Setup

A **25° decline**. Options, best first:

- A purpose-built decline board (inexpensive).
- A solid wedge or angled platform at the gym.
- The edge of a stair, heels off the edge (usually less than 25°).
- Two weight plates stacked under the heel (improvised, less standardized).

### How to perform it

1. Stand on the decline on the affected leg, **toes pointing down the slope, heel higher than toes.**
2. Lift the other leg slightly behind; use a wall or rail with **fingertips for balance only.**
3. **Lower slowly into a squat over 5 seconds**, to about **60° of knee flexion** (thigh approaching parallel; no deeper).
4. Pause briefly at the bottom, and return to standing over 2–3 seconds.
5. Do **5 reps**.
6. Rate the **worst pain at the lower edge of the kneecap** during the 5 reps, 0–10.
7. Repeat on the other side for comparison.

### When to do it

- **Once a week, on the same day each week.**
- **Fresh, not after a workout or run.** Mornings are best, before any warm-up masks tendon pain.
- Record both legs' scores in the week's Log.

### How to interpret it

- **0–2/10** on the affected side: **green**, progress.
- **3–4/10:** **yellow**, repeat the current week's running volume rather than progressing.
- **5+/10**, or rising week over week: **red**, drop back a week. If it persists 2 weeks running, see a sports-medicine clinician.
- The unaffected side is the runner's own baseline: across the rehab Phase, the affected side should come to match it.

A falling score over time is the **single best objective sign** that the loading is working.

## Pain rules for strength sessions

### Acceptable

- Up to **3/10 during lifting** that settles by the next morning.
- Mild morning stiffness that resolves within a few minutes.
- Muscle soreness (DOMS) for 24–48 hours after a session.

### Adjust the plan

- Pain over 3/10 during a lift, or **worsening across consecutive sessions of the same lift**: drop one RM step.
- A left/right strength gap persisting past week 6 of a rehab Phase: emphasize the weaker side and train it first.

### Stop and seek clinical evaluation

- Swelling at or around the tendon.
- Pain at rest, or pain that wakes the runner at night.
- A sudden pop, snap or tearing sensation.
- No improvement in the decline squat score after 6 weeks of compliant training.

---

## Timing and recovery

- During a 3-session rehab Phase, alternate days (e.g. Mon, Wed, Fri); in maintenance, two non-consecutive days.
- At least **48 hours** between heavy sessions.
- Heavy lifting after an easy run is fine; **never before** a hard run, race or long run.
- High-intensity running (intervals, hard tempo) competes with HSR for recovery: during a rehab Phase, pair heavy lifting with easy running rather than stacking it with interval work.

---

## What to record each session

With `log_strength_session`, one entry per exercise (which item from each slot, working load, sets and reps), with tempo and pain in the notes. In the session notes or the week's Log:

- Knee pain before, during and after (0–10, both sides), and the next morning, which matters most for tendon work.
- Overall effort (RPE 1–10) and any modification.

Weekly: the decline squat score for both legs, and the left vs. right working load on Slot 2. If the weaker side lags by more than about 15% past week 6 of a rehab Phase, flag it.

## Equipment

- **Essential:** barbell and plates (Slot 1), a range of dumbbells (Slots 2–4), a bench or sturdy 18–20" box (Slots 2 and 5), a resistance band (Slot 5), a cable column or hip abduction machine (Slot 4), and a 25° decline board for the weekly test.
- **At home:** goblet squat with the heaviest dumbbell on a wedge (Slot 1); Bulgarian split squats with dumbbells and a sturdy chair (Slot 2); single-leg RDL with a dumbbell (Slot 3); banded hip work, side-lying abduction and side planks (Slots 4–5); a stair edge or stacked plates for the decline test.

## Common questions

**"Can I do HSR on the same day as a hard run?"** No: it compromises recovery. Do it after easy runs or on rest days.

**"What if I miss a session?"** Continue with the next scheduled one. Don't make up missed sessions.

**"When do I add weight?"** When the working RM stops feeling like a true RM: every set and rep clean with reps in reserve. Add load next session.

**"Should HSR make me sore?"** Muscle soreness for 24–48 hours is normal, especially at first. **Joint pain is not muscle soreness**: follow the pain rules above.

**"Can I add more exercises?"** Quality over quantity. The 5-slot template covers what matters; more usually costs recovery without helping.
