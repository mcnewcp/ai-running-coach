# Strength Training Protocol — HSR-Based, Patellar-Tendon-Focused

> **Note (Jul 30, 2026):** The athlete's go-forward general strength program moved to a simpler movement-pattern framework — see [strength-training.md](strength-training.md). **This file remains active** for: (1) the **single-leg decline-squat test protocol** (still run weekly through Aug 16), and (2) the HSR history/reference for the 2026 patellar rehab block. The ~3s squat descent from HSR is carried into the new framework as the one tendon-protective habit worth keeping.

**Purpose**: Heavy Slow Resistance (HSR) loading for patellar tendon remodeling, plus integrated glute medius work for hip-control / knee-stability insurance.

**Frequency**: 3x per week during dedicated rehab blocks; 2x per week as ongoing maintenance once symptom-free.

**Tempo (Slots 1 & 2)**: **3 seconds down, 3 seconds up. No bounce, no pause at bottom.** This is the load that remodels the tendon.

This file is the durable, program-agnostic strength framework. Specific weekly progression and pain-gating live in the active program's plan file (see `data/current-goal.md`).

## Why This Protocol

### Tendons remodel under heavy, slow load
- Patellar tendinopathy is a **load problem, not a rest problem** — rest detrains the tendon and doesn't fix it.
- HSR (heavy resistance with slow, controlled tempo) stimulates collagen synthesis and improves tendon stiffness and load tolerance.
- Aggravators are **different** from therapeutic load: jumping, sprinting, downhill running, hill repeats (high strain-rate) and long fatigued runs (high cyclic volume) provoke; heavy slow lifting heals.

### Glute medius work is part of the prescription
- Weak/uncontrolled hip abduction is a contributing factor (often the root cause) of anterior knee pain in runners.
- Layered into every session as both rehab adjunct and long-term injury insurance — continue indefinitely after rehab blocks end.

## Session Structure

Every session uses the same **5-slot template**. Pick one item from each menu. Rotate within each menu across sessions to log variety.

### Slot 1 — Heavy bilateral knee-dominant (HSR core)

**Tempo: 3 sec down, 3 sec up. No bounce, no pause.**

- Barbell back squat
- Leg press
- Hack squat
- Goblet or front squat

### Slot 2 — Unilateral lower body

**Same 6-second tempo.** Start with the left (weaker) side, count reps, match the right side to whatever the left achieved.

- Bulgarian split squat (prescribe as "X lb × 2 DB" — one in each hand)
- Reverse lunge (DB or barbell)
- Walking lunge
- Step-up (18–20" box)
- Single-leg leg press

### Slot 3 — Hip hinge

Normal tempo (2 sec down, 1 sec up).

- Trap bar deadlift
- Romanian deadlift (barbell)
- Single-leg RDL (kettlebell or DB)
- Barbell hip thrust
- 45° hyperextension (weighted)

### Slot 4 — Glute medius main

Moderate-to-heavy load, **3 × 8–12 per side.** Lead with the left.

- Standing cable hip abduction (ankle cuff, low pulley)
- Side-lying hip abduction with dumbbell on outer thigh
- Single-leg hip thrust (DB or barbell on hip)
- Hip abduction machine

### Slot 5 — Glute medius finisher

Lighter load, **12–20 per side**, control-focused.

- Side plank with top-leg abduction (add DB for progression)
- Banded lateral walks ("monster walks"), 15 steps each direction
- Clamshell progression 4 (band, top knee raised, hip neutral)
- Lateral step-up onto 18" box
- Copenhagen plank

### Optional add-on
Standing or seated calf raises, **2 × 10–15.** Not required, but useful for runners.

## Understanding RM (Repetition Maximum)

**RM = Repetition Maximum.** Load expressed relative to your strength, not as a fixed weight.

- **1RM** = the heaviest weight you can lift for exactly one rep with good form. Rep two would fail.
- **15RM** = the heaviest weight you can complete for 15 reps. Rep 16 would fail.
- **6RM** = the heaviest you can do for 6 reps.

"3 sets of 15 at 15RM" means: pick a weight where the 15th rep is genuinely the last clean rep you could do. If rep 15 feels like you had 5 more in the tank, the weight was too light — add load on the next set.

No need to formally test 1RM. Estimate by feel, refine across sessions. After a week or two you'll know your RM for each lift.

## HSR Loading Progression (Slots 1, 2; Slot 3 optional)

Apply this curve to the heavy quad-dominant work (Slots 1 and 2). Slot 3 can follow the same curve. Slots 4 and 5 progress by feel — add weight or band tension when completing all reps cleanly.

| Weeks | Load | Sets × Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15RM | 3 × 15 |
| 2–3 | 12RM | 3 × 12 |
| 4–5 | 10RM | 4 × 10 |
| 6–8 | 8RM | 4 × 8 |
| 9–12 | 6RM | 4 × 6 |

**Pain rule during lifting:** Up to **3/10 during the lift is acceptable** and should settle by next morning. If pain is sharper than that, or worse session-to-session, **drop back one progression step** (e.g., return from 10RM to 12RM for a week).

## The Single-Leg Decline Squat Test

Standard clinical assessment for patellar tendinopathy. Used both diagnostically (confirm the tendon is the pain source) and as a **weekly progress tracker**. The decline angle puts the knee in deeper flexion at a given depth, increasing patellar tendon load — more sensitive than a standard squat for detecting tendon involvement.

### Setup

Need a **25° decline**. Options, best to worst:
- Purpose-built decline board (cheap on Amazon, ~$15–25)
- A solid wedge or angled platform from the gym
- The edge of a stair, heels off the edge (steepest stairs work best, but angle is usually <25°)
- Two weight plates stacked under the heel of a flat surface (improvise, less standardized)

### How to perform it

1. Stand on the decline with the affected leg, **toes pointing down the slope, heel higher than toes.**
2. Lift the non-affected leg slightly behind you, keep balance with wall or rail if needed (**fingertips for balance only, not weight bearing**).
3. **Slowly lower into a squat over 5 seconds**, aiming for about **60° knee flexion** (roughly thigh approaching parallel to the floor — no need to go deeper).
4. Pause briefly at the bottom, return to standing over 2–3 seconds.
5. Perform **5 reps**.
6. Rate the **worst pain felt at the lower edge of the kneecap** during the 5 reps, 0–10 scale.
7. Repeat on the unaffected side for comparison.

### When to do it

- **Once per week, same day each week** (e.g., every Saturday morning).
- **Done fresh, not after a workout or run.** Mornings are ideal — they remove the warm-up effect that can mask tendon pain.
- Log the score for both legs.

### How to interpret

- **0–2/10** on the affected side = **green light to progress.**
- **3–4/10** = **yellow** — repeat the current week's running volume rather than progressing.
- **5+/10** or pain increasing week-over-week = **red** — drop back a week. If it persists 2 consecutive weeks, see a sports-medicine clinician.
- The unaffected side is the personal baseline. Goal: across the rehab block, affected side gradually matches unaffected.

A reduction in this score over time is the **single best objective measure** that loading is working.

## Pain Rules — Strength Sessions

### Acceptable
- Up to **3/10 pain during lifting** that settles by next morning
- Mild stiffness on initial movement in the morning that resolves within a few minutes
- Muscle soreness/DOMS for 24–48 hours after a session — expected

### Adjust the plan
- Pain >3/10 during a lift, or pain **worsening across consecutive sessions of the same lift** → drop one RM step
- Left/right asymmetry persisting past week 6 of a rehab block → emphasize the weaker side, train it first

### Stop and seek clinical evaluation
- Swelling at or around the patellar tendon
- Pain at rest, or pain that wakes you at night
- A sudden pop, snap, or sharp tearing sensation during any activity
- No improvement in decline squat score after 6 consecutive weeks of compliant training

## Session Timing & Recovery

### Spacing within a week
- During a 3-day rehab block: Mon / Wed / Fri is standard (with appropriate running schedule)
- During maintenance (2x/week): non-consecutive days (e.g., Mon + Thu)
- Minimum **48 hours** between heavy strength sessions
- Sauna session (~20 min) post-session is recommended

### Strength + running integration
- Heavy lifting after an easy run is fine
- **Never before** a hard run, race, or long run
- High-intensity running (intervals, hard tempo) competes with HSR for recovery resources — pair easy running with heavy lifting; avoid stacking interval work with heavy lifting in the same rehab block

## What to Track Each Session

- Date and time
- Exercises performed (which item from each slot)
- Working load and tempo
- Reps × sets completed
- Knee pain rating before / during / after (0–10, both knees)
- Morning-after pain rating (0–10, both knees) — critical for tendon work
- Overall effort (RPE 1–10)
- Any modifications made

### Weekly trackers
- **Single-leg decline squat score** (both legs) — weekly, same day, fresh
- **Left vs right working load on Slot 2** — if left lags right by more than ~15% past week 6 of a rehab block, flag it

## Equipment

### Essential
- Barbell with plates (Slot 1)
- Dumbbells across a range (Slots 2, 3, 4)
- Bench or sturdy 18–20" box (Slots 2, 5)
- Resistance band (Slot 5)
- Cable column or hip abduction machine (Slot 4)
- 25° decline board for the weekly test (cheap, ~$15–25)

### At-home alternatives
- Slot 1: Goblet squat with heaviest available DB on wedge
- Slot 2: Bulgarian split squats with DBs and a couch / sturdy chair
- Slot 3: Single-leg RDL with DB
- Slot 4/5: Banded hip work, side-lying abduction, Copenhagen plank
- Decline board: stair edge or stacked plates under heel (less standardized)

## Common Questions

**"Can I do HSR on the same day as a hard running workout?"**
No. HSR after hard runs compromises recovery. Schedule HSR after easy runs or on rest days.

**"What if I miss a session?"**
Continue with the next scheduled session. Don't "make up" missed sessions.

**"How do I know when to increase weight?"**
When the working RM stops feeling like a true RM — i.e., you complete all sets/reps cleanly with reps in reserve. Add load on the next session.

**"Should HSR cause muscle soreness?"**
Yes, especially initially. DOMS for 24–48 hours is normal. **Joint pain is not muscle soreness** — see pain rules above.

**"Can I add more exercises?"**
Quality over quantity. The 5-slot template covers the critical areas. Adding more usually compromises recovery without improving outcomes.
